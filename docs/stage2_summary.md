# Stage 2 in one page: what the history says wins a Grand Prix

*Status: reviewed against the re-run outputs of October 2026 (who wins, race-pace ratings, dry-only pit loss, lap coverage). All Stage 2 outputs are now in and checked.*

## The short answer
1. **The car sets the order; qualifying is where it shows; the race adds a lot of noise.** In 2010-21 the car (team and season) explains 67% of the variation in qualifying position but 36% of finishing position. In 2022-now it is 40% and 27%. The driver explains 6% and 3% in 2010-21, and 16% and 8% in 2022-now (qualifying, race; rank measure). "Everything else" is 61-65% of the race result in the modern eras.
2. **Driver share is not a one-way decline.** In rolling ten-season windows the race driver share falls from about 9% (1950s) to 2-3% (1980s-2010s), bottoms at 4% qualifying / 3% race in 2010-19, and rises again to 13% / 5% in 2017-26. Whether part of the rise is arithmetic (cars closer together, same drivers) is not settled by the table of spreads in places: ranks always spread evenly over the field, so that table is the shares in other units (driver 0.9 to 1.6 places in the race, 1.4 to 2.3 in qualifying, 2010-21 to 2022-now, is the same finding as 3% to 8% and 6% to 16%). The lap-time table is in real units: qualifying driver share by lap-time gap is 14% (1990-2009), 3% (2010-21), 12% (2022-now), with the car at 63%, 78%, 38% and everything else at 24%, 19%, 50%. So the driver share still rises about four-fold from 2010-21 to 2022-now in real time, while the car share halves and 'everything else' more than doubles (a tighter field makes noise relatively bigger). This table has no stability ranges yet, and 2022-now has only 51 car-seasons.
3. **Pole is worth more than it used to be, because cars finish.** P(pole wins) = P(pole car finishes) x P(wins | finishes): 1950-69 68% x 60%; 1970-89 67% x 48%; 1990-2009 78% x 60%; 2010-21 92% x 56%; 2022-now 93% x 62%. The conversion given a finish has barely moved since 1990; the rise from 32% to 58% is mostly reliability.
4. **Grid order dissolves in the first 30-40% of the distance, then holds.** Dry races: rank correlation of grid vs running order 0.88-0.91 after lap 1, 0.73-0.77 at 40% of the distance, about 0.79-0.80 at the end. Late recovery in 2018-21 (+0.07, interval +0.05 to +0.10) is not clear since 2022 (+0.02, interval -0.00 to +0.04). Wet races end at 0.64.
5. **Pole is lost mostly in the pit cycle.** Since 2018 the pole sitter won 54% of 189 races with lap data. Of the losses: pit-stop cycle 47%, on-track pass 25%, safety car or red flag 10%, start 9%, leader retired 6%. No period shift is established (0 of 8 tests below 0.05).
6. **Rule changes did not move results by more than a normal year does** (5 changes tested, 30 comparisons, none survives correction). The test can only see swings of about 25-28 points in pole win rate or top-team share, so this is "no evidence", not "no effect". 2026 is untested (one season).
7. **Sprints** keep more grid order than a Sunday race (+0.14, interval +0.01 to +0.26) but about a third of that is distance: against Sunday at 30-40% distance the difference is +0.05 to +0.08 and includes zero. The car share is higher in sprints (41% vs 28%) with the same driver share (7%): removing stops, distance and most retirements removes "everything else", not driver credit.

8. **Winners mostly come from the front row, and more so now.** Pole sitter wins: 42% (1950-69), 32%, 46%, 51% (2010-21), 60% (2022-now). The quickest team (pole sitter or his team-mate, by qualifying) wins 58%, 45%, 56%, 68%, 65%. Winners from further back than the second-quickest team fall from 31% (1970-89) to 8% (2022-now). The winner out-qualified his single team-mate in 88% of 2022-now wins (81%-93%) against 75% in 2010-21 (69%-80%), but that is not a clean driver measure: starting ahead of your team-mate helps you win between equal drivers, and every pole win counts.
9. **Race pace, once the car is taken out, singles out one driver.** From 1,448 dry-race team-mate comparisons since 2018, Max Verstappen rates +0.81% of a lap faster than the average team-mate (interval +0.69 to +0.93), more than 0.5 points clear of anyone: Leclerc +0.27, Pérez +0.26, Norris +0.25, Alonso +0.22, all with overlapping intervals. The race and qualifying ratings agree at rank correlation 0.60 across 30 drivers; two differ by two standard errors (Pérez race +0.26 against qualifying -0.12; Russell race 0.00 against qualifying +0.40), which is what chance gives (1.5 expected). Rating error alone would give about 0.7-0.8, so some real difference between race and qualifying skill, or tyre age, strategy and traffic in the race laps, is likely.

## Where the numbers are soft
- Driver rankings (team-mate models) have overlapping intervals; read them as groups. They measure qualifying pace (1994 on) or grid places, not race craft.
- Tyre compound pace gaps are not separated (hard slower than medium in only 57% of races) and must not be used. Wear slopes are usable, but each compound is only seen where teams chose it. Circuit rankings of wear agree between random halves at only 0.40-0.44.
- Undercut: the 1-2 lap effect is +6 points (interval about 0 to +12); the 3+ lap figure reflects strategy, so "undercuts got stronger in 2022" is not supported.
- Pit loss: now dry-only (3,617 stops, median across circuits 22.4 s; Spa cheapest at 18.5 s, Imola dearest at 28.1 s). Wet-race stops are separate: median 25.2 s in 527 stops against 22.5 s dry.
- Race-pace ratings are not corrected for tyre age or traffic, so the order inside the top group is not settled; only Verstappen's lead is clear.
- One race (2018 Italian GP) has no lap data, so lap-based tables cover 189 of 190 races since 2018.

## Deep dive (October 2026): what was added after the review
Four modules answer the weakest points of the first pass. Numbers appear after they are run on the real data; none is quoted here until then.
- `what_decides`: the race result split into car, driver, qualifying form, mechanical failures and accidents (combined as "retirements" where a bare "Retired" status hides the cause, as in 2022-now), safety-car timing, strategy and unexplained, for 1950-69 to 2022-now. Strategy needs pit stops (2011 on), safety-car timing needs lap data (2018 on).
- `strategy`: team-mates who both finished on the lead lap, grid place controlled: places gained per extra stop, per lap of later first stop, per second of pit-lane time, per stop under a safety car.
- `car_driver_robust`: stress tests on invented worlds the model does not assume, driver skill against driver form, race-resampled intervals with an allowance for a wrong model.
- `answer`: the pieces side by side, read from the other outputs.


## Review of the deep-dive run (real data)

- Race result, 2022-now: car 28%, driver 8% (5-14% from resampled races; 4-16% allowing for hidden effects of the size the data show; 0-21% for deliberately strong ones), qualifying form 6%, strategy 2% (a floor), safety-car timing 1%, retirements about 29% together, unexplained 26%.
- The mechanical/accident split for 2022-now was unreliable because 224 retirements carry the bare status "Retired"; the build after this note keeps them apart and merges the two blocks where the cause is mostly unstated.
- Hidden effects found in the data are small: driver form 1-3%, driver-car fit 1-5%, in-season car development 0-2%. The basic model credits driver-car fit to the driver (2010-21 qualifying: about 5 of the 6 points). Ranking lowers the car share by about 3 points.
- Team-mates: one more stop costs about 2 finishing places (-2.02, -2.40 to -1.63; -1.92 with slow stops removed); this is mostly cars in trouble stopping more, not proof that fewer stops is a better plan.
